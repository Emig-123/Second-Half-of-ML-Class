Music Challenge¶

Which popular songs from the past will be recognized by future generations, and which will fade into obscurity? The website The Pudding conducts the Music Challenge where they ask thousands of people whether they recognize hit songs from the past. We'll look at some of the data related to the article "Defining the 90s Music Canon".

The data set at https://raw.githubusercontent.com/the-pudding/song-decay-clean/refs/heads/master/src/assets/data/lollipop_chart_data_july_13.csv contains 344 songs released in the 1990s. For each song, the proportion of survey respondents who recognized the song is recorded, for both Millennial respondents and Gen Z respondents. Our goal will be to use linear regression to predict the Gen Z recognition.

We will try several different linear regression models. Save each of your models with a different name because you will compare them at the end.

  1. Explain why you might want to consider taking a log of Gen Z recognition. Then use scikit-learn to fit a linear regression model for predicting log of Gen Z recognition from Millennial recognition.

Explanation: Recognition is a proportion and is right-skewed near zero. Modeling its log can reduce right skew and constrain back-transformed predictions to be positive. Because all observed recognition rates are positive, taking the natural log is defined. Note that exponentiating fitted log values gives predictions on the original scale that need not equal conditional means; it also does not guarantee predictions below 1.

Categorical Predictors (FEV data)¶

Do non-smokers have better lungs than smokers? One measure of lung function is forced expiratory volume (FEV), the amount of air (in liters) an individual can exhale in the first second of forceful breath. Larger values of FEV are indicative of better functioning lungs. In a study, FEV was measured for a group of 654 subjects, along with whether or not they smoked and other variables.

3. Look at the results for the previous parts. What do you notice? Is this what you expected? Remember: larger FEV means better lungs - who tends to have larger FEV, smokers or non-smokers?

Answer: In the unadjusted data, smokers have a higher mean FEV than non-smokers. This does not imply that smoking improves lung function: these are raw group averages without controlling for age and height, and smoking status was not randomly assigned.

4. Suggest some explanations for why smokers tend to have higher FEV than non-smokers?

Answer: Smokers in the sample may be older and taller, and lung capacity tends to increase with physical growth in children and adolescents. Thus, age and height are confounders that can create a misleading positive association between smoking and FEV. The data are observational, so this comparison cannot establish a causal benefit from smoking.

Answer: The smoking-only model is confounded by age. The age-adjusted models are more informative because they compare people at similar ages; the interaction model additionally allows the age–FEV slope to vary by smoking status. However, adding predictors almost always improves training fit, so I would compare their cross-validated RMSE and consider whether the interaction meaningfully improves out-of-sample predictions. I would also test a model including height, inspect residuals, and avoid treating smoking coefficients as causal effects in observational data.
