Classification Models¶

Discussion questions¶

Suppose you are developing a classification model to determine whether or not a person has a particular disease (based on symptoms, results of diagnostic tests, family history, risk factors, etc).

  1. Explain what "precision" represents in this context.

Precision: Among the people our classifier predicts have the disease, the proportion who truly have it: $TP/(TP+FP)$. High precision means relatively few false alarms.

  1. Explain what "recall" represents in this context.

Recall: Among people who truly have the disease, the proportion the model correctly identifies: $TP/(TP+FN)$. High recall means relatively few missed cases.

  1. Which of the following do you think is a more serious error? It's an opinion question, but choose one and discuss.
  • Concluding that a person has the disease if they actually don't have it
  • Failing to conclude that a person has the disease if they actually do have it

My choice: failing to identify a truly infected patient (a false negative). Missing a case could delay treatment and allow the disease to worsen. The seriousness depends on the disease and costs of additional testing or treatment; false positives can also cause harm.

  1. Given your answer to the previous question, which would you prefer to be higher: precision or recall?

Recall. Given my concern about false negatives, I would prioritize detecting as many truly diseased patients as possible.

  1. Suppose you want to achieve higher recall. Should your classification rule be more or less strict about who it predicts to have the disease?

Less strict. Lowering the decision threshold for a positive prediction generally increases the number of people classified as having the disease, which tends to increase recall.

  1. What is a downside to your choice in the previous part?

Downside: A less strict decision rule usually increases false positives (decreasing precision). More healthy patients may undergo unnecessary further testing, treatment, anxiety, or expense.

import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from pathlib import Path
from sklearn.pipeline import make_pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler
from sklearn.neighbors import KNeighborsClassifier
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression
from sklearn.model_selection import StratifiedKFold, cross_validate, GridSearchCV
from sklearn.metrics import ConfusionMatrixDisplay

cv10 = StratifiedKFold(n_splits=10, shuffle=True, random_state=42)
metrics = {"precision_macro": "precision_macro", "recall_macro": "recall_macro", "f1_macro": "f1_macro"}

def summarize_cv(model, X, y, title):
    scores = cross_validate(model, X, y, cv=cv10, scoring=metrics, n_jobs=1)
    out = {name: round(scores[f"test_{name}"].mean(), 4) for name in metrics}
    print(title, "(10-fold mean scores):", out)
    return out

if df_train is not None:
    features = ["fixed acidity", "volatile acidity"]

    # In scikit-learn, the inverse-penalty hyperparameter C=1 corresponds to stronger
    # regularization than larger C values. 'liblinear' supports binary L1 logistic regression.
